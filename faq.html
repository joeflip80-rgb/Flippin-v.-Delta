Peptide FAQ

Quick, plain-English answers to the questions people ask most.

What is a peptide?

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids — the building blocks of proteins — linked by peptide bonds. The body uses many peptides as signaling molecules: tiny messengers that tell cells what to do, such as regulating blood sugar or triggering tissue repair.

What does "reconstitution" mean?

Reconstitution is adding a measured liquid — usually bacteriostatic water — to a freeze-dried (lyophilized) peptide powder so it dissolves into a measurable solution. Our calculator turns those amounts into syringe units.

What's the difference between mcg and mg?

One milligram (mg) equals 1,000 micrograms (mcg). Peptide doses are often described in micrograms because the amounts are very small.

What is bacteriostatic water?

Sterile water containing a small amount of preservative (typically 0.9% benzyl alcohol) that inhibits bacterial growth, allowing a reconstituted vial to remain usable over time. It differs from sterile water for injection, which has no preservative.

How do I read peptide research without being misled?

Always ask "studied in what?" A result in cells or mice is far from a proven human benefit. Watch for cautious wording like "may" and "in preclinical models," check sample sizes and controls, and separate seller marketing from primary research. Our beginner guide covers this in detail.

Are peptides legal?

It depends entirely on the specific peptide and your country or region. Many peptides discussed online are research chemicals not approved for human use. Always check your local laws and consult a qualified professional.
